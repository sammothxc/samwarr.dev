Ever since I participated in a semester long project building custom mechanical keyboards completely from scratch as part of the IEEE club at BYU, I'd been looking for opportunities to build something a little more complex than just a keyboard. When thinking about my dad's upcoming birthday, the perfect thing came to mind. My dad is an accountant, and growing up I always saw him using the same tax calculator. I'm sure he still has it. While it serves its purpose, I never thought the keys were that nice to type on and beyond basic tax macros, that calculator doesn't come with some of the more advanced functions that can be found on more expensive calculators or even in software suites.

And that's when it hit me, I could combine my PCB design and keyboard knowledge to build "the ultimate" calculator. It could be (re)programmable with any function that math has so far discovered and be mechanically switched for the best comfort that keyboarding has to offer. It would utilize Cherry MX switch hot-swap sockets so that my dad could easily customize the switches to his liking. The perfect mix of keyboard fun and microcontroller fun.

I put a GREAT deal of effort into squeezing in every possible feature that I could think of that would be of use for my dad into the Tactical Tenkey. I decided on the Seeed XIAO ESP32S3 as it has everything I needed to make this project work, nothing more or less. Some of the most attractive features: a deep sleep mode with ultra low power consumption (a mere 14uA), Bluetooth 5, battery charging circuit, and USB-HID compatibility. Fitting it all into the form factor of a numpad would not only make it easier and cheaper to get a nice set of comfortable keycaps, but also allow my dad to use it as an actual tenkey numpad when connected to my dad's laptop via the USB-C port or via bluetooth on battery power when he's on the go.

Speaking of on the go, I decided to use an 18650 cell since they are easy to get ahold of, high capacity, and my dad could easily replace it if needed (compared to a soldered-on pouch cell of a smaller capacity). I spent over a month just testing and tuning the battey life and the worst I got was 27 straight hours with the display at max brightness and bluetooth actively connected. The high end is theoretically the self-discharge rate of the battery (so hundreds of hours when in deep sleep mode) and the average life ended up varying between 50 and 80 hours. That's probably more than my dad will ever need but he travels lots so if he were to accidentally leave a USB-C cable behind on a week-long business trip, the Tactical Tenkey wouldn't let him down.

For the display, I wanted something with good contrast but it also couldn't be harsh on my dad's eyes. With the help of my wife (who has a more keen and sensitive eye than I do), we sampled 9 different types of displays. It was a bit of an interesting journey, but we eventually settled on a white 2.42 inch OLED with a resolution of 128x64. The excellent contrast of an OLED was very apparent with the white on black of this display, and the 2.42 in size made it more readable than almost any other size of readily available display. The problem with white versus other colors is that it is slightly harsher on the eyes than I was hoping for. This was fixed with an unexpected solution- using the same tinted acrylic that we were using for the rest of the case and putting the display behind it. With the display shining through the acrylic, it make it much more comfortable to look at and still readable at indoor light levels. In bright outside light its not the best, but knowing my dad and where he normally does his work, this was quite an edge case and wasn't really a problem.

Just when it felt like everything was coming together, the problems started rolling in. The first one came with the battery charging circuit on the ESP32S3. To save costs, the ESP32S3 module battery management system is capable of charging a battery and nothing more. It does not have anything to stop the battery from being drained too much and killing it, or from being overcharged and possibly starting a fire. I had to learn about battery management circuits like the DW01 and 8205S. That meant soldering tiny SOT-23-6 components by hand, with pads barely HALF of a millimeter thick.

The next problem was with the custom ordered PCBs (thru JLCPCB). I mistakenly left out a through-hole on one of the GPIO pads on the bottom of the ESP32S3, which meant I had to resort to spending an hour wrestling with a hot plate soldering station in BYU's soldering lab to fix that mistake.

The last big issue was getting the OLED display to be bright enough behind the tinted acrylic. It was just a tad too dim, so I ended up having to push my little desktop 3018 CNC to its precision limits by machining out the inside of the acrylic to be thinner right where the display is so that the tint wasn't as strong. I thought this was a pretty cool solution, as the outward facing side of the faceplate appeared to be perfectly smooth, and the cutout entirely concealed.

With the hardware finally together, it was time to focus on the firmware. This was going to make or break the project, as a crappy firmware would make this otherwise perfect gadget useless. My dad told me that in the financial world, a good calculator needs 12 digits. In addition to that, I wanted a small operation history line (for example: `4 + 8 * 9 /`) and a dedicated status bar to display things like battery status, numpad or calc mode, USB or bluetooth. Since the calculator has the ability to imitate a numpad via USB-HID, I added a key combination to send the result of a calculation to the computer, useful for when my dad is auditing and needs to input a result into a spreadsheet or email. Why type it in two different devices when the calculator can just "send it" to the computer by typing it in for you?

But the biggest selling point of the entire project: macro programmability. By default, the calculator comes with a number of built-in tax macros for performing advanced and/or repetitive calculations. They can be quickly activated by `Fn`+`0-9` key combo or by selecting one out of a pop-up menu. Once selected, the operation history line now becomes a prompt line for the input (for example: `Enter principle amount:` or `Enter the number of units per shipment:`). After all inputs have been entered and the result(s) calculated, the Tactical Tenkey can display multiple results by using the history line to label them (for example: `Total revenue: 153,517,957` and `Profit Margin: 65`).

The cool part about this is that I'm NOT an accountant, and I don't have to be. My dad can simply request a macro that takes any number of inputs and spits out any number of outputs. I can program that in the firmware, push it to the GitHub repo, and all he has to do is visit [my self-hosted web app](https://t2.samwarr.dev) to flash the latest firmware to give his Tactical Tenkey any number of new features and functionality.

At the time that I am writing this, I haven't yet sent the calculator to him. I expect that it'll be a tough choice for him between his long time, trusty, battle tested tax calculator and the Tactical Tenkey but even if it doesn't get daily accounting action, I hope this fun project can show him my appreciation for all he has done for me as my dad, one of my best friends, and the greatest role model who I will always look up to. 

Love you Dad!
